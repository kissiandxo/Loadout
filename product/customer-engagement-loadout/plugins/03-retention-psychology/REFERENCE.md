# Plugin: Retention Psychology

A reference for the psychological drivers behind why customers stay, leave, or come back. Practical patterns — not academic theory. Loaded into your project, this gives every retention-related agent a richer understanding of human behaviour to draw from.

## When to use this plugin

Pair with:
- **cancellation-save** (understanding what's actually driving the cancel decision)
- **winback-campaign** (understanding what would actually pull them back)
- **nps-csat-followup** (interpreting what scores mean beneath the surface)
- **onboarding-sequencer** (designing for retention from day one)
- **vip-customer-manager** (understanding why loyal customers stay loyal)

## The 5 retention drivers (ranked by strength)

Most retention conversations focus only on price and product quality. The actual drivers are deeper.

### 1. Identity alignment ("This is for people like me")
The strongest retention driver. Customers stay when the brand reflects how they see themselves. Patagonia customers don't stay for the jackets — they stay because Patagonia matches their self-concept as someone who cares about the planet.

**Practical implications:**
- Customer's review / referral language reveals their identity claim (notice what THEY say they are when they recommend you)
- Brand voice should match the customer's identity, not aspirational marketing personas
- A customer who's stopped seeing themselves in you is gone, even before they cancel

### 2. Sunk cost + switching cost
Customers stay because they've invested time/data/setup in your tool. Not a great reason but a real one.

**Practical implications:**
- The first 30 days are where you build switching costs (data, integrations, custom setup) — onboarding that creates "stickiness"
- BUT high switching cost without identity alignment creates resentment — they stay but leave reviews
- The right framing isn't "you can't leave" but "look how much you've built here"

### 3. Habit / routine
Customers stay because using you is now part of how their week runs. They don't think about whether to use you — they just do.

**Practical implications:**
- Habit forms in the first 60-90 days through repeated usage
- Daily-use products have higher retention than weekly which has higher than monthly
- Breaking the habit (extended absence, vacation, work change) is when you lose them — proactive re-engagement during predictable absences (holidays, etc.) prevents this

### 4. Specific value delivered recently
Customers stay because something you did for them recently is fresh in their mind. The last positive interaction is worth more than 6 months of background satisfaction.

**Practical implications:**
- A great support interaction in the last 30 days is one of the strongest retention signals
- A bad interaction in the last 30 days is one of the strongest churn signals
- "Last touch" beats "all touches" in retention impact
- This is why VIP outreach with no specific occasion still works — it counts as a positive touch

### 5. Price-value gap is acceptable
The weakest driver, despite being the most discussed. Customers can rationalise a higher price IF the other four drivers are strong. They cancel when the price seems indefensible relative to what they're getting.

**Practical implications:**
- "Too expensive" almost always means "I don't feel I'm getting enough value" — fix the value perception, not the price
- Lowering the price without addressing perceived value train cancellers to expect discounts
- Pricing is the last lever to pull, not the first

## The 5 churn drivers (ranked by frequency, not severity)

### 1. Indifference (the silent churner)
- They didn't hate you, they just stopped engaging
- Usage tailed off over weeks/months
- Eventually noticed they weren't using it, cancelled

**Recovery angle:** Re-establish habit OR step out of their way (pause, downgrade). Don't try to argue them back into engagement they never asked for.

### 2. Bad experience accumulation
- Each individual bad experience was tolerated
- The combined weight tipped them over
- Often triggered by ONE additional small thing that wouldn't matter alone

**Recovery angle:** Acknowledge the pattern, not just the trigger. "Three things went wrong in a row" beats "sorry about this latest one."

### 3. Life circumstance
- Customer's situation changed (job, finances, family, business closure)
- Has nothing to do with your product
- Often returns when circumstances change again

**Recovery angle:** Make leaving easy and dignified. Don't chase. Maintain a light-touch open door.

### 4. Better alternative emerged
- Found a competitor that solved a specific pain better
- Often it's a new entrant or a competitor that finally caught up

**Recovery angle:** Understand the specific pain. Either fix it (and re-approach) or accept the loss.

### 5. Pricing pressure
- Decided the price isn't justified given current usage / value
- Often comes after a price increase OR after usage decline

**Recovery angle:** Downgrade option (best). Discount (worst — trains future cancellers).

## The customer journey moments that determine retention

Three specific moments matter disproportionately:

### Moment 1 — First-week impression
- Did they accomplish the "aha" action?
- Did they have a positive support touch (or no support touch needed)?
- Did the product match their pre-purchase expectations?

**If yes to all three:** ~85% retention to 90 days
**If no to any:** ~40% retention to 90 days

### Moment 2 — First friction event
- Something went wrong (bug, billing issue, confusion, frustration)
- How it was handled determines whether they stay long-term

**Handled well:** Often INCREASES retention vs customers who had no friction (counter-intuitive but documented)
**Handled poorly:** Likely terminal — bad first-friction events rarely recover

### Moment 3 — Habit-break
- Vacation, role change, project ended, etc. — they didn't use you for 2+ weeks
- Returning OR not returning is binary

**Proactive re-engagement during predictable absences:** ~70% return
**No re-engagement:** ~35% return

## Specific retention plays that work

### Play A — The "month 3 surprise" gesture

At month 3 of customer tenure, send a small unexpected gesture (free month / free feature / handwritten note / small gift). The cost is small. The retention impact is significant because it's UNEXPECTED — customers aren't psychologically pre-paying for it like a launch discount.

### Play B — The "fail forward" recovery

When something goes wrong, over-resolve the situation specifically. Customer who experiences ONE great recovery often becomes more loyal than customers who never experienced failure. The recovery itself is the brand moment.

### Play C — The "founder direct line" for VIPs

Top-tier customers getting access to the founder directly creates loyalty that's hard to replicate. Even unused, the OFFER signals trust. Used occasionally for genuine value, it locks in the relationship.

### Play D — The "anti-feature" honest moment

Tell your customers what you're NOT good for. ("If you're a 50-person team needing X, we're probably not the right fit yet.") Counterintuitively builds trust because it signals honesty.

### Play E — The "graduate" framing

At 6 months / 12 months, frame the customer as having "graduated" from the new-customer phase. Send a personal note marking the milestone. People stay where their identity is reinforced.

## Specific retention plays that DON'T work (despite being popular)

### Anti-play A — Generic "we miss you" emails
- Treats churn as a relationship problem when it's usually a product/value problem
- Adults find emotional manipulation transparent

### Anti-play B — Discount escalation in cancel-flow
- "10% off!" → "no?" → "25% off!" → "no?" → "free month!"
- Trains customers to threaten cancellation for discounts
- The customers who accept are the ones least likely to retain long-term anyway

### Anti-play C — Loyalty programs with points
- Generic SaaS-style loyalty points have low engagement
- Customers don't change behaviour for accumulating numbers
- The exception: programs that give genuine progression (status tiers, real perks)

### Anti-play D — Surveys without action
- Sending NPS surveys without acting on responses worse than not asking
- "We hear you, we'll work on it" without specifics damages trust

### Anti-play E — Personalised marketing that's clearly automated
- "Hi {{first_name}}, we noticed you bought {{product_name}}…" reads as gross
- Worse than generic — at least generic is honest about being generic

## Behavioural signals that predict churn (in priority order)

1. **Sudden drop in usage** — best single predictor
2. **Increase in support tickets followed by silence** — frustration giving way to disengagement
3. **Cancellation of related auto-renew settings** — subtle prep for full cancellation
4. **Last login >30 days for daily-use products / >90 days for monthly** — habit broken
5. **Failed payment they didn't resolve quickly** — disengagement signal
6. **Removed integrations / disconnected accounts** — pulling out their data
7. **Downgrade to lower plan** — sometimes prelude to full cancellation, sometimes life-saving for the relationship

## How to use this plugin

1. Drop this REFERENCE.md into your customer engagement project knowledge
2. When using any retention-focused agent, Claude will draw from these patterns
3. Use the "5 retention drivers" and "5 churn drivers" as a diagnostic in customer conversations — which of the 10 is in play?
4. Reference the "moments that determine retention" when designing onboarding, recovery flows, or VIP programs
5. Avoid the anti-plays even when they feel tempting — they don't work at the population level even when they occasionally pay off individually

## What NOT to do

- **Don't apply retention plays without understanding the customer.** A first-time customer who churned in week 2 needs different handling than a 2-year customer who quietly drifted away.
- **Don't treat churn as a single problem.** Five drivers, different responses.
- **Don't ignore identity alignment in favour of more measurable drivers.** It's the strongest one even though it's the hardest to measure.
- **Don't over-rely on discount-based retention.** It works short-term and damages long-term unit economics.
- **Don't assume churn is rational.** Most churn decisions are made emotionally and rationalised post-hoc.
